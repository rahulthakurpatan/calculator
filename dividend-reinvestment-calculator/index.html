How dividend reinvestment (DRIP) accelerates wealth

Dividend reinvestment means using a stock or fund's cash distributions to acquire additional shares rather than receiving the money as spendable cash. Those new shares may themselves qualify for future dividends, increasing the number of income-producing shares without additional external contributions. Brokers and issuers may offer formal dividend reinvestment plans (DRIPs), with specific purchase times, costs, fractional-share rules and eligibility conditions. The US Securities and Exchange Commission describes DRIPs as an option for using cash dividends to buy more of the same stock. Investor.gov DRIP guidance.

Reinvestment is not a source of free value. The value distributed as a dividend comes out of a business or fund, and share prices often adjust around the ex-dividend date. The value in DRIP is the potential compounding of subsequent investment returns on dividend-funded shares, together with any new money you invest. Actual share prices can rise or fall, dividends can be reduced or stopped, and the entire projected growth path is hypothetical.

DRIP versus taking dividends as cash

With DRIP enabled, this model converts each month's modeled dividend into new fractional shares at the projected month-end share price. With DRIP disabled, the dividend is recorded as a cash payout and is not reinvested or compounded. The page displays both the stock market value and the combined economic wealth if those cash payouts are retained without earning interest. If you spend cash distributions, only the remaining stock value is still invested. The DRIP-versus-cash chart compares like-for-like ending wealth, including retained dividend cash in the payout scenario.

Understanding dividend yield versus dividend growth rate

Dividend yield is the annual dividend per share divided by the current share price. For example, a $100 share paying $3.50 per year has a 3.5% dividend yield. Dividend growth is the assumed annual percentage change in the dividend paid per share; price appreciation is the separate modeled change in the share price. When dividend-per-share growth is faster than price appreciation, the forward yield can increase over time. When price growth is faster, the forward yield can decline even though the dollar dividend rises. A high quoted yield is not a guaranteed high return, as Fidelity's dividend-yield guidance also explains. Dividend yield explained.

The projection calculation in plain English

Starting shares = initial investment ÷ normalized starting share price (100)
Monthly share price = starting price × (1 + annual price growth)month / 12
Annual dividend per share at month start = starting price × starting dividend yield × (1 + dividend growth)(month − 1) / 12
Monthly dividend = shares held × annual dividend per share ÷ 12
New DRIP shares (if enabled) = dividend paid ÷ projected month-end price
Final stock value = shares held × ending share price

The model purchases new contribution-funded shares at the beginning of each modeled month (or each new year for annual contributions), and adds dividend-funded fractional shares at month-end. A month's dividends apply to shares purchased by that month's contribution. The hypothetical price path represents price-only appreciation and treats dividend payouts separately; it does not simulate the market's specific ex-dividend-day price adjustment, tax lots, split dates, trading fees or corporate actions. Real dividend frequencies may be quarterly, semi-annual or annual instead of monthly.

Calculating total returns with recurring contributions

With a single initial investment and no later deposits, a simple end-value CAGR can summarize the annualized growth of combined stock and retained dividend cash. With monthly or annual contributions, however, treating every contributed dollar as if it were invested at the start gives the wrong performance figure. This calculator uses a monthly cash-flow internal rate of return (IRR), converts that rate to an annual equivalent and treats the ending stock plus retained payout cash as the terminal value. This measures the modeled investor experience, including contribution timing. It is a money-weighted rate, not a time-weighted comparison benchmark. The CFA Institute's GIPS performance methodology distinguishes these return approaches. GIPS return methodology.

Pro tips for building sustainable passive dividend income

  • Review dividend coverage. Cash flow, earnings quality, leverage and payout policy matter more than a headline yield alone.
  • Stress-test dividend cuts. Try a negative dividend growth rate, such as −5% or −10%, rather than assuming every company raises payouts indefinitely.
  • Track total return. Market value can decline while dividends keep arriving; income alone does not measure overall investment success.
  • Understand taxes and DRIP costs. Reinvested dividends may still be taxable depending on the account and country; broker plans can have fees or restrictions.
  • Diversify intelligently. A concentrated high-yield portfolio may expose an investor to company, sector, currency and dividend-policy risks.

Frequently asked questions

How does dividend reinvestment (DRIP) work?

When dividends are paid, a DRIP uses them to purchase additional shares. Those shares can then generate further dividends. Brokers may have purchase rules, trading fees and fractional-share restrictions; this tool assumes automatic monthly purchases without fees.

What is the difference between dividend yield and dividend growth?

Yield relates the current annual dividend per share to the current stock price. Dividend growth is the change in the payout per share over time. The price may change independently, causing the future yield to be different.

Are dividends guaranteed, and does a high yield mean high returns?

No. Companies can reduce or suspend payouts and share prices may fall. A temporarily high yield may reflect a falling stock price, so assess total return and financial sustainability.

How is annualized return calculated when I contribute monthly?

The tool calculates a money-weighted internal rate of return using month-by-month contributions and ending total wealth. Dividing final wealth by all deposits and calling the result CAGR would incorrectly ignore when the deposits occurred.

Are dividends taxed if I reinvest them?

They can be, depending on the account, type of distribution and country. Reinvestment itself does not make a distribution tax-free. The model excludes tax, trading costs, inflation, foreign exchange and unplanned dividend cuts.

What is a Systematic Withdrawal Plan (SWP)?

Understand how a mutual fund SWP works before estimating your withdrawals.

A Systematic Withdrawal Plan (SWP) is a facility offered by mutual funds that allows an investor to redeem a chosen amount from an existing investment at regular intervals. Depending on the instruction and scheme rules, withdrawals may be scheduled monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annually.

An SWP is not interest paid by the fund and it is not an assured pension. The fund pays the requested amount by redeeming mutual fund units at the applicable redemption NAV. The units left after redemption remain invested and their value can rise or fall with the market.

How a monthly SWP works

  1. You invest a lump sum or already hold mutual fund units.
  2. You register a withdrawal amount and frequency with the fund house or platform.
  3. On each scheduled date, units worth the payout are redeemed at the applicable NAV.
  4. The remaining units continue to participate in the scheme's gains and losses.

For example, if the requested payout is ₹20,000 and the applicable NAV is ₹50, the redemption is approximately 400 units before considering any applicable charges or scheme-specific rules. If NAV is lower, more units are needed for the same rupee payout.

SWP calculator formula and methodology

Transparent calculation logic, including withdrawal timing and compounding.

1. Convert annual return into a monthly rate

Monthly rate = (1 + Annual return)^(1/12) − 1

The calculator converts an effective annual assumption into an equivalent monthly rate. It does not simply divide the annual percentage by 12. For a quarterly, half-yearly or yearly SWP, the balance is still simulated month by month and withdrawals are grouped at the selected frequency.

2. Apply growth and scheduled withdrawals

Closing corpus = Opening corpus × (1 + monthly rate) − withdrawal

This formula represents an end-of-month withdrawal. With start-of-period timing, the withdrawal is deducted before that month's growth. If the remaining corpus is smaller than the scheduled payout, the model pays only the remaining balance and marks the corpus as exhausted.

3. Increase withdrawals annually

Withdrawal in year n = Starting withdrawal × (1 + annual step-up)^(n − 1)

The step-up applies after every 12 months. A 5% annual increase means the scheduled payout rises by 5% at the start of each new projection year.

4. Show inflation-adjusted value

Value in current purchasing power = Future value ÷ (1 + inflation)^years

This is a purchasing-power illustration. It does not change the nominal withdrawal amount unless you separately enter an annual withdrawal increase.

5. Estimate units redeemed

If you enter a starting NAV, the calculator grows that illustrative NAV at the same constant monthly return and estimates units redeemed as payout divided by the modelled NAV. Real fund NAVs fluctuate independently; this is not a prediction of actual units or redemption statements.

Worked SWP example

Suppose an investor starts with ₹50 lakh, plans to withdraw ₹30,000 every month and assumes an 8% annual return for 20 years. The first-year planned withdrawal is ₹3.6 lakh, equal to 7.2% of the initial corpus. That percentage is only a cash-flow ratio—not a safe withdrawal rate.

The final corpus depends on the return sequence, withdrawal dates, step-up and actual fund performance. Use the calculator above to change one assumption at a time. A projection with a positive ending balance does not prove that the same income will be sustainable in real markets.

SWP tax, costs and important limitations

Know what this calculator includes—and what it deliberately does not pretend to calculate.

Is the full SWP amount taxable?

Not automatically. A mutual fund withdrawal is a redemption of units. The redemption proceeds can include the cost of acquisition (principal component) and a capital gain component. Tax is generally assessed on the applicable capital gain, not simply on the entire cash payout as if it were interest.

The actual calculation can depend on scheme classification, purchase dates and lots, holding period, acquisition cost, investor status, realised gains and the tax law applicable to the transfer. Different units redeemed in the same SWP can have different holding periods and costs. Refer to the Income Tax Department's capital gains guidance and consult a qualified tax professional for your circumstances.

Tax is not included in the result. This calculator shows a pre-tax cash-flow projection. It does not calculate capital gains tax, surcharge, cess, exit load, STT, expense ratio or other charges. Do not treat the result as post-tax income.

Exit load, expense ratio and scheme rules

Some schemes may apply an exit load for redemptions within a specified period. Scheme rules, minimum redemption amounts, cut-off times, settlement and bank-credit dates can also affect the actual payout. The model excludes these scheme-specific details.

Why market sequence matters

A constant 8% return every month is not how markets behave. Two portfolios can earn the same long-term average return but produce different SWP outcomes if one experiences losses early while withdrawals are being made. Selling more units during a downturn can leave fewer units to participate in a later recovery. This is commonly discussed as sequence-of-returns risk.

Mutual fund investments are market-linked and are not guaranteed or assured-return products. NAVs can rise or fall, and you may receive less than the amount invested. Read the scheme documents and risk factors. See AMFI's mutual fund risk information.

Frequently asked questions about SWP

Practical answers about withdrawals, returns, tax and retirement income.

What does SWP stand for?

SWP stands for Systematic Withdrawal Plan. It is a mutual fund facility through which units are redeemed at regular intervals to pay a selected amount to the investor.

Is SWP better than a dividend or IDCW option?

They are different mechanisms. An SWP is an investor-initiated redemption of units. IDCW distributions depend on the scheme's distributable surplus and are not assured. Compare cash-flow needs, tax treatment, scheme risk and total return rather than assuming one is always better.

Can I withdraw every month from a mutual fund?

Many mutual fund schemes offer periodic withdrawal facilities, but available frequencies, dates, minimum amounts and processing rules depend on the scheme and fund house. Confirm the current scheme terms before registering.

Will my SWP continue if the fund makes a loss?

A scheduled redemption may still be processed subject to scheme rules and available units, but a lower NAV means more units may be redeemed for the same rupee amount. Repeated withdrawals during a downturn can reduce the remaining corpus faster.

Can I increase my SWP every year?

You can usually change the withdrawal instruction with the fund house or platform, subject to its process. This calculator models an annual step-up in your chosen percentage; it does not submit or change a real SWP instruction.

How much can I withdraw monthly from ₹10 lakh?

There is no universal monthly amount that is safe for every investor. It depends on the time horizon, asset allocation, return sequence, inflation, tax, costs and how much capital you are willing to use. Enter ₹10 lakh in the calculator and compare conservative and adverse assumptions rather than relying on one return figure.

Does a higher expected return always make an SWP safer?

A higher assumed return can improve a deterministic projection, but it is not guaranteed and usually comes with risk. A smooth average-return model does not capture volatility, market crashes or sequence risk.

What happens when the corpus reaches zero?

The model stops the projection at the month the available balance is exhausted. If the scheduled withdrawal is larger than the remaining balance, only the remaining amount is counted as paid.

Does the calculator include tax?

No. It shows pre-tax estimates. SWP redemptions can realise capital gains, and tax depends on the fund category, acquisition lots, holding periods and applicable law. The calculator does not know your purchase history or tax profile.

Are SWP returns guaranteed?

No. Mutual fund returns and NAVs are market-linked. An SWP is a withdrawal facility, not a guaranteed interest or pension product. You can lose capital.

What is the difference between SWP and SIP?

A SIP invests money at regular intervals, while an SWP redeems money from an existing mutual fund holding at regular intervals. One is a contribution process and the other is a withdrawal process.

Does this calculator recommend a mutual fund?

No. It does not select funds, assess suitability or provide investment advice. It is a general-purpose mathematical planning tool.

What is net worth?

Net worth is the difference between what you own and what you owe. Your assets may include cash, investments, retirement accounts, property and vehicles. Your liabilities include outstanding mortgages, loans, credit-card balances and other debts.

How to calculate net worth

Add the current value of each asset, add the outstanding balance of each liability, and subtract total liabilities from total assets.

Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities

For example, if your assets total $320,000 and your liabilities total $135,000, your net worth is $185,000. Use your own reporting currency and current balances.

Total net worth vs liquid net worth

Total net worth includes all assets, including property and vehicles. Liquid net worth focuses on assets that may be easier to convert to cash, such as bank balances and marketable investments. This calculator treats cash, stocks, ETFs, mutual funds and retirement accounts as liquid categories by default; you can edit asset labels, but the liquid estimate is a practical category-based approximation rather than a valuation of how quickly an asset can be sold or withdrawn without cost.

How the future net worth projection works

The calculator simulates each month. Asset values grow using the annual rate entered for each asset row. Monthly contributions are added to an investment asset, with the annual contribution increase applied over time. Debt balances accrue interest monthly and are reduced by the payment entered for each liability. The resulting future net worth is projected assets minus projected liabilities.

Inflation-adjusted net worth

A future amount may buy less than the same amount today. The calculator divides projected net worth by the compounded inflation assumption to show an approximate value in today's purchasing power.

Scenario comparisons

Conservative, expected and optimistic scenarios use the same starting balances, contributions and debt payments. They vary the annual growth assumptions by the scenario adjustment you choose. They are illustrations, not probability ranges.

How to reach a net worth milestone

Choose a target such as $100,000, $250,000 or $1 million. The milestone timeline checks the expected monthly projection and identifies the first month the projected net worth reaches or exceeds your target. If the target is not reached within the selected projection period, the calculator says so.

Important limitations

  • Enter all balances in one selected currency; no exchange-rate conversion is performed.
  • Asset growth rates are assumed to be constant within each scenario and do not model volatility, taxes, fees or transaction costs.
  • Property, vehicle and private-business values are estimates and may not be quickly saleable.
  • Debt payments are treated as fixed monthly payments; new borrowing, variable rates, fees and payment changes are not modelled.
  • Investment contributions are assumed to be made at month-end and allocated to one investment asset row.
  • Retirement account withdrawal restrictions and tax consequences are not included.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate net worth?

Add your current assets and subtract all outstanding liabilities. Use current market values and current debt balances.

Should I include my home in net worth?

Yes, include its estimated current market value as an asset and the remaining mortgage as a liability. The difference is your home equity.

What is liquid net worth?

It is a practical estimate of cash and more readily marketable assets minus relevant short-term liabilities. It is not the same as total net worth.

Does the calculator connect to my bank or investment account?

No. Enter values manually. Calculations run in your browser, and optional history snapshots are saved only in local browser storage.

Can I use this calculator for negative net worth?

Yes. If liabilities exceed assets, current net worth will be negative. The projection can show when the balance becomes positive and whether a chosen target is reached.

Are future net worth results guaranteed?

No. Results depend on assumptions you enter. Actual market returns, property values, inflation, interest rates, taxes and personal circumstances can differ materially.

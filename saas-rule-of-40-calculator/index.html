What is the SaaS Rule of 40?

The Rule of 40 is a shorthand for the balance between a subscription company's growth and profitability. Under the SaaS Metrics Standards Board's recommended definition, add the company's annual recurring revenue (ARR) growth percentage to its free cash flow (FCF) margin percentage. A combined score of 40% is the conventional reference, not an industry law or a predictor of company value. Different investors sometimes substitute EBITDA margin. This calculator lets you select the FCF-recommended or EBITDA-based variant explicitly, while cash burn is assessed separately.

Rule of 40 and Burn Multiple formulas

ARR growth (%) = (Ending ARR − Beginning ARR) / Beginning ARR × 100 ARR bridge (optional) = New ARR + Expansion ARR − Contraction ARR − Churned ARR Free Cash Flow = Operating cash flow − Capital expenditures FCF margin (%) = Free cash flow / Recognized annual revenue × 100 EBITDA margin (%) = Annual EBITDA / Recognized annual revenue × 100 Rule of 40 = ARR growth (%) + Selected FCF or EBITDA margin Net new ARR = Ending ARR − Beginning ARR Annual net cash burn = max(0, −FCF) OR direct annual cash burn Burn Multiple = Positive annual net cash burn / Positive net new ARR Simple cash runway (months) = Available cash / (Annual cash burn / 12)

All changes and cash flows must cover the same 12-month observation period. ARR is a snapshot of expected annualized recurring revenue at a specific date; it is not the same as revenue recognized through the past twelve months. Financing raised or borrowed should not be counted as operating revenue or recurring business growth.

A burn multiple is not meaningful if net-new ARR is zero or negative, or if the company is producing cash rather than burning it. In these scenarios the calculator displays N/A or "No burn" rather than inventing a zero or negative multiple.

Worked example: SaaS with 50% ARR growth

Consider a company moving from $1.2 million beginning ARR to $1.8 million ending ARR over twelve months. ARR growth is 50%. Its recognized annual revenue is $1.45 million, while operating cash flow is negative $150,000 and capital expenditures are $90,000. Therefore FCF is −$240,000, giving FCF margin around −16.6%. The Rule of 40 score is about 33.4%—below the usual 40% reference.

With $600,000 of net new ARR and $240,000 of annual cash burn, the burn multiple is 0.40×. A $950,000 cash reserve implies a simple annualized runway near 47.5 months at the historical burn rate. This cash runway is not a robust liquidity forecast: salaries, capital expenditures, churn, seasonal collections and financing needs may change considerably.

Why calculate Rule of 40 and Burn Multiple together?

The Rule of 40 rewards aggregate growth and margin performance. Burn Multiple answers a narrower question: how many dollars of net cash the company consumed for each dollar of net-new ARR. Both are worth examining because a founder can grow quickly while using cash inefficiently—or grow more slowly but with unusually low burn. Neither metric should replace cohort retention, net revenue retention, customer concentration or gross margin analysis.

What is a good SaaS burn multiple?

There is no universal threshold for every stage of a company. David Sacks popularized a benchmark in which lower burn multiples indicate more capital-efficient ARR expansion. A result below 1× means less than one dollar of net cash burn per dollar of net ARR added; higher results suggest more funding needed for growth. Early-stage businesses and fast-growing mature SaaS firms can have very different economics. Avoid interpreting generic cutoff bands as lending or valuation advice.

How the Rule of 40 sensitivity chart works

Change the ARR growth sensitivity input to model up to five potential growth scenarios around your current observed ARR growth. In each scenario, the tool holds the selected FCF or EBITDA margin constant and recomputes the Rule of 40. It does not assume that faster growth is free or that cash expenses would remain unchanged; the chart is intentionally an isolated sensitivity test.

Which Rule of 40 metric should you choose: FCF or EBITDA?

The SaaS Metrics Standards Board recommends FCF margin alongside ARR growth. EBITDA-based versions also appear in investor analysis, but EBITDA and free cash flow can differ substantially because EBITDA excludes changes in working capital and capital expenditure. Choose one definition, label it and avoid mixing it with benchmarks that use different methods.

How to analyze ARR new, expansion, contraction and churn

Net-new ARR equals new customer ARR plus expansion ARR, less downgrade/contraction and churned ARR. The ARR bridge option derives ending ARR from these amounts so net-new ARR and annual growth use a consistent balance. The calculation does not infer movements from other metrics or count new sales before they become recurring revenue.

Comparing Rule of 40 across reporting periods

The optional prior-year comparison takes your previous ARR growth rate and your previous profitability margin under the same definition. The difference is a percentage-point movement in Rule-of-40 score, not a predictive growth rate. A rising score may reflect improved margins, faster growth or both.

Common mistakes in SaaS capital-efficiency analysis

  • Comparing ARR to cash revenues: The FCF margin uses annual recognized revenue, but ARR growth uses comparable ARR snapshots 12 months apart.
  • Burn-period mismatch: Dividing one quarter of cash burn by twelve months of ARR growth produces a misleading burn multiple. This calculator uses a consistent annual interval.
  • Ignoring churn: Net new ARR must reflect expansion and lost subscriptions, not gross new sales alone.
  • Overstating runway: A simple cash balance divided by trailing monthly burn is not a cash-flow forecast.
  • Assuming strong Rule of 40 means profitability: High growth can outweigh a negative cash margin.
  • Confusing cash with EBITDA: FCF includes operating cash and capital expenditures. EBITDA may diverge substantially.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use EBITDA margin or free cash flow margin?

FCF margin is the SaaS Metrics Standards Board's recommended Rule of 40 profitability measure, while EBITDA margin is an alternative. Compare scores only when both periods or companies use consistent definitions.

Can Rule of 40 be above 40 if the SaaS business is losing money?

Yes. A company growing ARR at 70% with a negative 20% FCF margin scores 50%; it can be cash-negative but still beat the conventional Rule of 40 benchmark.

What if SaaS ARR has declined?

The ARR growth percentage becomes negative. If the company also burns cash, burn multiple is not a useful ratio because net new ARR is nonpositive. The dashboard correctly marks it not applicable.

Is burn multiple the same as burn rate?

No. Burn rate describes cash consumed over time; burn multiple compares cash consumed with net ARR growth over the same period.

Can I use this calculator in the UK, Canada or India?

Yes. Select a single consistent reporting currency. The ratios are dimensionless, so the same accounting definitions apply. This tool does not perform foreign-exchange conversion, enforce accounting standards or calculate local corporate taxes.

Is the Rule of 40 a reliable valuation formula?

No. It is a performance heuristic, not a valuation multiple or a prediction of company worth. See our separate SaaS valuation calculator for scenario-based estimates.

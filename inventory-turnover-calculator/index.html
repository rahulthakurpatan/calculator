What is inventory turnover?

Inventory turnover measures how many times cost-valued stock is sold or consumed during a reporting period. It is calculated using cost of goods sold (COGS), not sales revenue. Days inventory outstanding (DIO), also called days sales of inventory (DSI), expresses the same relationship in days. A fast turnover can reduce cash tied up in stock, but excessively low stock levels can create stockouts and lost sales.

Average inventory = (beginning inventory + ending inventory) / 2 Period turnover = period COGS / average inventory Annualized turnover = period turnover × 365 / period days DIO = period days / period turnover Target average inventory = annualized COGS / target annualized turnover Potential cash release = max(0, current average inventory − target average inventory) Annual carrying cost = average inventory × annual carrying rate Potential annual carrying-cost savings = max(0, current − target annual carrying cost)

Worked example: from 6 to 8 turns

For a 365-day year, suppose COGS is $600,000, opening inventory is $90,000 and closing inventory is $110,000. Average inventory is $100,000, annual turnover is 6.00× and DIO is 60.83 days. At an 8.00× annual target and unchanged COGS, target average inventory is $75,000. The difference is $25,000 of potentially released stock investment, and at a 20% annual carrying rate the indicative annual cost saving is $5,000. This is a steady-state planning estimate, not a guaranteed reduction in operating expenses or a cash-flow forecast.

Why the period matters

When COGS covers a quarter, enter the quarter's days (for example 91), not 365. Period turnover is COGS divided by average inventory; annualized turnover multiplies that by 365 / period days to make comparisons easier. The DIO result uses the original period, so quarterly data do not inflate the inventory days by four times. Annualization assumes the measured pace persists and may mislead seasonal businesses.

What is a good inventory turnover ratio?

There is no universal ideal. Perishables, consumer electronics, apparel and specialty machinery have very different sales cycles and stocking requirements. Compare against similar products, industry peers and your own trend. Too-low turnover can mean slow-moving or obsolete stock; too-high turnover may signal insufficient safety stock, stockouts or expensive emergency replenishment.

How to use the target scenario

Enter your desired annualized turnover and annual carrying-cost rate. The model holds annualized COGS constant and calculates the inventory base implied by that target. It shows potential stock cash released only when target inventory is below current average inventory; if the target requires more stock, the cash-release metric is zero and the comparison table still shows the increased stock requirement. Carrying-cost estimates are based on a user-entered rate; they exclude tax, financing structure and changes to demand. Do not cut stock below practical reorder and safety-stock needs.

Limitations and data quality

Beginning and ending inventory are a simple two-point average. For a seasonal operation, averaging monthly inventory snapshots is often more representative. COGS and inventory must use compatible accounting cost bases and the same currency. If COGS is zero, or average inventory is zero, turnover and DIO cannot be meaningfully calculated by this tool. The result does not measure gross margin, revenue growth, cash conversion cycle or business profitability by itself.

Frequently asked questions

Should I enter sales or COGS?

Use cost of goods sold, because inventory is valued at cost. Using sales revenue exaggerates the turnover ratio by including markup.

Can I use a 90-day period?

Yes. Enter 90 as period days and COGS for those same 90 days. The calculator displays both period and annualized turnover separately.

Is reducing inventory always good?

No. Inventory reduction can increase shortages, delivery delays and lost sales. Evaluate service levels and supplier lead times before acting on a target.

How overtime and time-and-a-half pay work (FLSA standards)

Overtime is additional pay for eligible hours beyond the applicable standard. For many covered, nonexempt US employees, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) generally requires at least one-and-a-half times the employee's regular rate for hours actually worked over 40 in a fixed workweek. A workweek is seven consecutive 24-hour periods and does not need to match a calendar week. Averaging hours across two workweeks is not allowed to avoid overtime. Paid leave or holiday hours that were not actually worked usually do not count toward the federal 40-hour overtime threshold. US Department of Labor overtime guidance.

This calculator is designed for a single hourly pay rate with a weekly overtime threshold. Your employment contract, state or provincial rules, industry-specific provisions, union agreements or collective bargaining arrangements can differ. In California, for example, overtime may apply to hours beyond eight in a single day and double time beyond twelve, subject to exceptions. A weekly-total-only calculator cannot determine California's daily entitlements. California overtime guide.

Time-and-a-half versus double-time

At 1.5×, an hourly rate of $20 pays $30 for each overtime hour. At 2×, it pays $40. Double time is not a general federal FLSA requirement. UK workers, for example, have no automatic legal right to overtime premium rates; the contractual terms and minimum-wage protections must be checked. Acas overtime guidance.

Overtime pay formula with an example

Regular hours = min(total weekly worked hours, weekly threshold)
Overtime hours = max(0, total weekly worked hours − weekly threshold)
Regular pay = regular hours × hourly wage
Overtime pay = overtime hours × hourly wage × overtime multiplier
Gross pay = regular pay + overtime pay

Example: suppose you earn $25 per hour, work 48 hours in a week and qualify for 1.5× overtime after 40 hours. The regular component is 40 × $25 = $1,000. Overtime pay is 8 × $25 × 1.5 = $300. Gross pay is $1,300. Of the $300 for overtime hours, $200 is normal wages and $100 is the incremental half-time premium. The payroll treatment of bonuses, commissions or shift differentials can require adjustments to the regular rate under the FLSA. DOL regular-rate guidance.

How to calculate your blended hourly rate

A blended hourly rate shows the average gross earnings earned for every hour actually worked, including the overtime uplift. Divide total gross pay for the week by total worked hours. Using the example above, $1,300 ÷ 48 = $27.08 per hour, rounded to two decimal places. It is different from your contractual base rate of $25 and different from your overtime-specific rate of $37.50. If there are zero hours worked, the blended rate is not applicable.

The annual projected gross amount multiplies one repeated weekly gross figure by 52. A bi-weekly projection uses two matching weeks; a semi-monthly estimate uses annual wages divided by 24; a monthly projection divides annual wages by 12. Actual paychecks depend on the number of workweeks and worked hours in each payroll period. For legal overtime purposes, calculate individual workweeks first — do not average hours across several weeks.

Pro tips for tracking overtime and understanding earnings

  • Keep a daily work log. Record start times, end times, unpaid breaks and actual hours worked. Daily records matter where daily overtime rules apply.
  • Confirm the workweek. Ask your employer when the fixed seven-day workweek begins and whether your position is classified as overtime-eligible.
  • Check the regular rate. Some nondiscretionary bonuses and commissions must be included in the FLSA regular-rate calculation, not simply ignored.
  • Separate gross wages from take-home pay. Income taxes, social security, benefits and other deductions affect the amount deposited into your account.
  • Make realistic projections. Annual estimates assume the same weekly hours all year. Account for reduced shifts, holidays, unpaid time away and seasonal variation when budgeting.

Does the 2026 US overtime deduction mean overtime is tax-free?

No. Under the current US rules, some individuals may qualify for a federal income-tax deduction for eligible overtime premium amounts required under FLSA section 7, subject to limits and eligibility. Overtime wages are not automatically excluded from gross income, and tax withholding and employment taxes can still apply. This gross-pay calculator does not estimate eligibility, deductible amounts or net tax savings. See the August 2026 IRS update and current tax guidance before filing.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate time-and-a-half overtime pay?

Multiply eligible overtime hours by your hourly wage and 1.5, then add pay for regular hours. At $25 an hour and 48 hours worked with a 40-hour threshold, overtime pay is $300 and weekly gross pay is $1,300.

Is overtime always paid after 40 hours?

No. US federal FLSA generally uses 40 hours per workweek for covered, nonexempt workers. State and sector requirements, exemptions, collective agreements and international law may differ. California can require overtime based on daily hours.

What is the difference between overtime pay and overtime premium?

Overtime pay means the total wages for overtime hours at the selected multiplier. Overtime premium is only the extra amount over straight-time wages. With eight hours at $25 and 1.5×, overtime pay is $300 and the extra premium is $100.

How is a blended hourly rate calculated?

Divide total gross pay by actual hours worked. At $1,300 over 48 hours, the blended rate is about $27.08. With no worked hours, the blended rate is not applicable.

Does this tool calculate taxes or my exact paycheck?

No. It estimates gross earnings for a single hourly rate and a weekly overtime threshold. It does not calculate withholding, benefits, union premiums, daily overtime, bonuses, exemptions or exact semi-monthly calendars.

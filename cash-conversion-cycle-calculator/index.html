What is the cash conversion cycle?

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) estimates the time between paying suppliers and collecting cash from customers. It combines days inventory outstanding (DIO), days sales outstanding (DSO) and days payables outstanding (DPO). A lower CCC generally means less operating cash is tied up, but comparisons depend on the industry and the company's operating model.

Average balance = (beginning balance + ending balance) / 2 DSO = average trade receivables / period credit sales × period days DIO = average inventory / period COGS × period days DPO = average trade payables / period COGS × period days Cash conversion cycle = DSO + DIO − DPO Cash released by lower DSO = days reduced × (period sales / period days) Cash released by lower DIO = days reduced × (period COGS / period days) Cash released by higher DPO = days increased × (period COGS / period days)

Worked example with beginning and ending balances

For a 365-day period, assume sales of $1,200,000, COGS of $720,000, average receivables of $150,000, average inventory of $90,000 and average payables of $60,000. DSO is 45.625 days, DIO is 45.625 days and DPO is about 30.417 days. The CCC is about 60.83 days. Reducing DSO by five days would release an estimated $16,438.36, assuming sales remain unchanged.

How this tool differs from a simple CCC calculator

Instead of using one year-end snapshot, this calculator takes beginning and ending inventory, receivables and payables, then averages each balance. It lets you change the period length for quarterly or custom periods and tests three independent operational levers. Cash release is estimated separately using sales for receivables and COGS for inventory/payables to avoid mixing bases.

Practical ways to improve the cycle

Consider prompt invoicing and collections for DSO, demand forecasting and obsolete-stock reviews for DIO, and agreed supplier credit terms for DPO. Do not stretch payables beyond contractual terms merely to improve the metric. A negative CCC can be normal for some business models; it is not automatically proof of financial health.

Important assumptions

Use net credit sales for DSO when available; if you enter total revenue instead, the DSO result is an approximation. For DPO, trade payables should relate to the COGS base; differences in supplier purchases, labor and inventory accounting can distort comparisons. When COGS is zero, nonzero inventory or payables cannot yield meaningful DIO/DPO and the calculator asks you to correct the input. It does not predict cash flows, financing costs or credit eligibility.

Frequently asked questions

Can the cash conversion cycle be negative?

Yes. If suppliers allow longer payment terms than the combined inventory and collection period, the cycle can be negative.

Why use average balances?

Beginning and ending balances reduce the distortion of a single reporting-date snapshot, though more frequent averages may be better for highly seasonal businesses.

Does reducing DSO always release the estimated amount?

No. It is a simple steady-state estimate, not a cash-flow forecast. Sales mix, disputes, seasonality and bad debts can change the outcome.

How to calculate the inventory reorder point

The reorder point (ROP) is the inventory position at which your business should place a replenishment order. It is not the quantity to buy. For example, a store selling an average of 35 units per day with a nine-day supplier lead time expects to need 315 units while it waits for delivery. Carrying only those 315 units could create stockouts if customers buy more than usual or the supplier is delayed. Safety stock adds a statistical buffer to that baseline.

This free reorder point calculator includes both demand variability and supplier lead-time variability. You can paste recent daily sales and let the tool calculate their mean and sample standard deviation, or enter forecast numbers directly. The right panel updates automatically, without a form submission.

Reorder point and safety stock formulas

We use a continuous-review, independent-demand, normal lead-time-demand approximation. The statistical model is consistent with the combined variability methodology described by the Association for Supply Chain Management (ASCM).

Mean lead-time demand = d × L Lead-time demand SD = √(L × σd² + d² × σL²) Safety stock = ceil(Z × lead-time demand SD) Reorder point = ceil(d × L + Z × lead-time demand SD) Inventory position = On hand + On order − Backorders − Committed Reorder if inventory position ≤ reorder point

Where d is average daily demand, L is average supplier lead time in calendar days, σd is the standard deviation of daily demand, σL is the standard deviation of lead time, and Z is the one-sided normal Z-score for the selected cycle service level. The model assumes independent demand variation between days and independent supplier lead-time variability. It may be unsuitable for highly seasonal, intermittent, promotional, correlated or non-normal demand.

We round safety stock and the reorder threshold upward to whole units, since a business cannot stock fractional units of a discrete product. This protects against underestimating the threshold through conventional rounding.

How to decide when to reorder inventory

Do not compare the reorder point to physical stock alone if outstanding supplier orders or unfulfilled customer requests exist. Instead, use inventory position: stock on hand + on order − backorders − committed units. The tool flags “Reorder now” when that position is at or below the reorder threshold. Be sure that “on order” units can arrive in time; this simplified calculator does not track a shipment-by-shipment arrival calendar.

The suggested purchase quantity is a planning heuristic, not an economic order quantity (EOQ) optimum. When reorder is triggered, the tool aims for a target inventory position equal to the reorder point plus average demand over an extra coverage period. It then rounds up to the supplier pack multiple, with a minimum of one pack on a trigger. Adjust the extra coverage days to reflect your review cadence, storage capacity, order minimums, and supplier economics. For precise EOQ optimization, include ordering and annual carrying costs in a separate purchasing model.

Worked example: small online store

Suppose your shop sells 35 items on an average day, with daily demand SD of 12 units. Your supplier takes nine days on average, with a two-day lead-time SD. At a 95% cycle service target, expected lead-time demand is 315 units. Combined variation produces a lead-time demand SD of about 78.7 units. Safety stock is rounded up to 130 units, producing a reorder point of 445 units.

If you have 360 units on hand, 60 already ordered, and 15 backordered, your inventory position is 405 units. Since that is below the 445-unit threshold, the calculator recommends reordering. With 14 extra target coverage days and a 12-unit pack multiple, the suggested order is 540 units (target position 935 minus current 405, rounded up in packs). These are illustrative decisions, not recommendations for any particular warehouse.

Limitations of the inventory model

  • Cycle service level is not fill rate. A 95% cycle service goal refers to avoiding stockouts in roughly 95% of replenishment cycles under the model, not fulfilling exactly 95% of demanded units.
  • Normal variability is an approximation. Very slow-moving items, spikes, seasonal effects, changing trends and correlated supplier delays need a more detailed model.
  • Time units matter. Daily demand and lead times both use calendar days here. Convert working days and weekly data consistently before entering.
  • Stock arrival timing matters. Inventory position assumes incoming orders are relevant to the replenishment policy, but the tool cannot know individual shipments’ arrival dates.
  • Order size is not EOQ. It is a rounded order-up-to estimate. Supplier minimum order amounts, shelf life, cash limits, warehouse capacity and discounts may change the appropriate order size.
  • *Days-until-threshold is only a straight-line projection. It assumes average demand continues and no inventory receipts change the position.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a reorder point and safety stock?

A reorder point includes average demand while waiting for delivery plus the extra buffer. Safety stock is only that buffer, designed to reduce the chance of running out during uncertain demand or lead times.

What if my supplier always delivers on the same day?

Set lead-time SD to zero. The model becomes safety stock = Z × daily demand SD × √(lead time). If daily demand SD is also zero, the calculated statistical safety stock becomes zero.

What is a good reorder point for an e-commerce store?

There is no one-size-fits-all number. Demand rate, demand variation, delivery reliability, current commitments, service goals and product-specific costs determine the result. Review the threshold after major changes in demand or supply.

Do I reorder when stock on hand reaches the threshold?

Use inventory position rather than stock on hand alone in a continuous-review model. Include pending purchase orders and deduct outstanding backorders and committed units, while verifying the expected receipt dates.

Why does increasing service level increase reorder point?

The Z-score rises as you request higher stock availability. A larger statistical buffer is needed to protect more replenishment cycles against unusually high demand or late deliveries.

Can I use this tool for multiple SKUs?

Run it separately for each SKU. A distinct service target and demand history for each product is usually more useful than assuming a single threshold across a store. The CSV export saves the current scenario.

401(k) employer match calculator with vesting and job-change analysis

An employer match is additional money your company may contribute to your workplace retirement plan when you save from your paycheck. But a 401(k) match is not always dollar-for-dollar, and it is not always immediately vested. Your employer might match 100% of the first 3% of compensation, 50% of the next 2%, impose a fixed annual cap, or use an entirely different formula. The match could be calculated per pay period, and making all contributions early can reduce the total match without a true-up.

This calculator combines three decisions in one place: (1) How much match are you receiving? (2) How much employee deferral could capture the full modeled employer match? (3) How much of the employer-funded balance would you own if you leave your employer at a specific number of credited service years? Unlike an all-purpose retirement calculator, it focuses on the matching rule and vesting schedule rather than treating all employer contributions as automatically yours.

How the matching calculation works

Eligible salary = min(annual salary, IRS compensation cap) Employee elective deferral = min(salary × selected deferral %, employee annual deferral limit) Tier 1 eligible employee savings = min(elective deferral, eligible salary × tier 1 band %) Tier 2 eligible savings = min(max(0, elective deferral − tier 1 band), eligible salary × tier 2 additional band %) Raw employer match = Tier 1 savings × tier 1 match rate + Tier 2 savings × tier 2 match rate Employer matching allowed = min(raw match, optional plan dollar cap, remaining IRC 415 annual-additions room) Vested employer account = (existing employer account + estimated current-year match) × vesting percentage Potentially forfeitable employer account = Total employer account − vested employer account Employee-owned balance = existing employee-funded account + current-year employee deferral

The tool considers the selected year's employee deferral limit, applicable catch-up limit, annual additions limit and eligible compensation cap. It excludes other employer nonelective contributions, after-tax employee amounts, forfeitures allocated by the plan and deferrals made to other employers' retirement plans, all of which can reduce actual available limits. It assumes the standard 401(k) plan design, not SIMPLE 401(k), governmental 457(b) or specialized catch-up rules.

401(k) contribution limits for 2026 and 2027

On November 12, 2026, the IRS published its 2027 retirement-plan cost-of-living adjustments in Notice 2026-66. The basic employee 401(k) limit rose from $24,500 in 2026 to $25,500 for 2027. Different catch-up thresholds apply when the participant is age 50–59, age 60–63, or age 64 or older by year-end. These figures are not a guarantee your employer's own plan permits the full allowed contribution.

Worked example: 100% of the first 3%, 50% of the next 2%

Assume a $80,000 salary and a two-tier matching plan. You contribute 4% of pay, or $3,200 per year. Your employer matches all of the first 3% of salary ($2,400) and half of the next 1% of salary ($400). Total employer match: $2,800. If you increase your deferral to 5% ($4,000), the second tier rises to $800 and the total employer match reaches $3,200. You therefore miss $400 of potential annual employer contributions at a 4% election.

This calculation assumes that the plan applies the formula to the full year's compensation and contributions, including any required plan true-up. If the employer matches every paycheck separately and does not true up at year-end, the actual match can differ depending on when you contribute and whether you max out too early.

What does 401(k) vesting mean if I change jobs?

The IRS distinguishes between employee elective deferrals, which are always 100% vested, and employer contributions, whose ownership can be restricted under a plan's vesting schedule. For a traditional defined contribution plan, two commonly permitted maximum schedules are a three-year cliff and six-year graded vesting. Other plans provide faster vesting, including immediately. Some safe-harbor contributions are required to vest immediately, while other plan types or contribution categories may use different provisions.

Example: After three credited years in a graded plan, you own 40% of eligible employer money. If your current employer-funded balance including this year's projected matching contribution is $20,800, an estimated $8,320 may be vested and $12,480 may be subject to forfeiture when you leave. Your own salary deferrals and investment earnings on those deferrals remain your property. The actual outcome depends on your plan document, vesting service calculation, termination rules, and whether the employer has made the expected contribution.

Official vesting references: IRS Retirement Topics – Vesting, IRS vesting schedules for matching contributions.

How vesting years differ from calendar years

A "year of vesting service" is determined under the plan's crediting method. Some plans require a minimum number of hours during a service-counting period, while others use different legally permitted approaches. A person who joined in July may have three calendar anniversaries without being credited three full vesting years under a specific plan. Check your Summary Plan Description, plan administrator or employer benefits statement before relying on an exit calculation.

Does employer matching count toward the 401(k) contribution limit?

Employer matching generally does not count against the employee elective deferral cap (for example, $25,500 in 2027), but it does count toward a separate annual additions limit alongside non-catch-up employee contributions and certain other sources. This calculator considers that second limit, but because it does not collect every possible source of additions, it cannot replace payroll limit validation or your plan's individual annual statement.

Why the full-match percentage may not be exactly 5%

The standard 5% example depends on your employer's tier bands. If the plan matches 50% of employee savings up to 6% of compensation, the employee generally needs to defer 6% to capture the full planned match. If the plan instead pays a flat match, has an employer dollar cap, or the IRS deferral limit reduces eligible contributions for a highly compensated participant, the required percentage can change. The calculator searches the tier breakpoints and contribution limits to find the lowest annual contribution amount that reaches the model's maximum employer match.

2026–2027 Roth catch-up and other exceptions

Beginning in 2026, certain higher-paid participants are generally subject to special Roth treatment for catch-up contributions depending on prior-year FICA wages and applicable transition guidance. This calculator does not determine whether your catch-up must be Roth, model federal or state taxes, or check nondiscrimination, hardship withdrawal, employer true-up policies or plan-specific compensation exclusions.

What does the long-term projection mean?

The optional graph compounds existing account balances alongside hypothetical future contributions and matches. Salary grows at the rate you enter, and balances use an assumed annual return converted into a monthly rate. To avoid inventing future IRS rules, it holds the selected 2026 or 2027 statutory limits constant across the entire projection. Actual annual adjustments, vesting, employment changes, investment fees, salary changes and market losses can make future values substantially different. The plotted total account is not necessarily a vested amount at every future year.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate 401(k) employer matching?

Apply the match formula to the eligible salary-deferral dollars in each pay band. For example, a 50% match on the first 6% of salary yields up to 3% of eligible pay as employer contributions. A two-tier formula adds each matched band, then applies any dollar cap or statutory restrictions.

Is the 401(k) employer match free money?

It is an employer-provided benefit, but plan participation, eligibility, matching timing, vesting, taxes and market changes matter. If you leave before employer contributions vest, you may lose their unvested portion.

Do employee 401(k) contributions ever vest slowly?

No. IRS guidance says employee elective deferrals and their investment earnings are always fully vested. The graded or cliff vesting selection in this calculator applies only to the employer-funded side.

What if my plan offers immediate vesting?

Choose "Immediate (100%)" in the vesting dropdown. The estimate then treats the employer-funded balance as fully owned, ignoring other plan-specific eligibility and contribution timing conditions.

Does this work for a SIMPLE 401(k) or a safe harbor plan?

The basic matching calculations may resemble those plans, but the statutory limits, contribution requirements and vesting can differ. This tool's IRS limit table is for a standard 401(k), and it is not a compliance calculator for a SIMPLE plan. Confirm your plan type.

How do I avoid losing employer match by contributing too early?

If your employer matches per paycheck without a year-end true-up, front-loading can exhaust your deferrals before all matching periods occur. Spread contributions according to the plan's payroll rules, and ask the plan administrator about any true-up.

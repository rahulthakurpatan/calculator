How invoice factoring works

Invoice factoring is a way for businesses to receive money sooner from unpaid customer invoices. A factoring provider typically advances a percentage of the invoice immediately. The rest is held in reserve until the customer pays, at which point the provider releases the remaining balance less agreed fees. Factoring may be recourse (the business bears some customer non-payment risk) or non-recourse under specified contract terms.

Example: A business has a $50,000 invoice. A factor offers an 85% advance, a 2.5% fee for each started 30-day period, and a $150 processing charge. If the customer pays in 45 days, two fee periods are charged, so the provider's fee is $2,500 plus $150 in other charges. The business receives $42,500 upfront, with $4,850 released after payment. Total proceeds are $47,350, before other possible contract costs.

Transparent formulas and methodology

For the inputs in this calculator, the simplified cash flow is:

Advance = Invoice × Advance rate
Billing periods = 1 (flat), days / 30 (prorated), ceil(days / 30), or ceil(days / 7)
Factoring fee = Fee base × Quoted rate × Billing periods
Total fees = Factoring fee + Other fees
Reserve settlement = Invoice − Advance − Total fees
Total net proceeds = Invoice − Total fees
Simple annualized cost = Total fees / Advance × 365 / Days × 100

The annualized cost uses the amount you can use immediately (the advance), not the full face value. This makes it useful as an illustrative comparison, but it is not a legally disclosed APR. Some jurisdictions prescribe an actuarial APR method and other financing disclosure conventions.

Three questions before signing a factoring agreement

  • Is the quoted percentage charged once or repeatedly? A 2% fee per started 30-day period may double after day 30, even if your customer pays on day 31.
  • What happens if the reserve is smaller than the fees? Some agreements may require additional settlement payments, offsets, or minimum charges. A negative reserve settlement in this calculator is a warning that the advance and fees cannot both be funded by the invoice itself.
  • Who bears default and dispute risk? Recourse provisions, collections practices, lockboxes, minimum volume requirements, and termination charges can matter more than the headline fee.

Invoice factoring vs a business line of credit

Both can accelerate working capital, but they work differently. Factoring generally involves selling an account receivable, while a line of credit involves borrowing against creditworthiness and sometimes collateral. This calculator compares illustrative dollar financing costs for the same advance and duration. It does not compare approval odds, tax treatment, accounting classification, collections administration, credit risk, or access to either product. A lower modeled interest cost does not necessarily make a bank product available to your business.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate factoring fees on a $50,000 invoice?

Multiply the amount to which your provider applies the fee by the quoted rate and the number of fee periods. Then add wire, processing, or administrative charges. For a 2.5% charge on the whole invoice billed twice, the factoring fee alone is $2,500.

Is a 3% factoring fee the same as 3% APR?

No. A 3% fee may apply to the full invoice for just 30 days while the cash advanced is less than the face value. The simple annualized cost can therefore be much higher than 3%. Different regulation-specific APR conventions may apply to actual disclosures.

What is a reserve in invoice factoring?

A reserve is the portion of the invoice not advanced immediately. The factor commonly releases it after customer collection less its fee and any other amounts owed under the agreement.

Can factoring cost less than a line of credit?

It can under some quotes and collection periods. Use the comparison as a starting point, then check the provider's agreement, expected collection times and any separate expenses. Neither option is guaranteed to be available.

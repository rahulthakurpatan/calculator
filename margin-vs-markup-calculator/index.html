What is the difference between margin and markup?

Both start with the same gross profit, but they answer different pricing questions. Gross margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price; markup is profit as a percentage of the direct cost. The terms are not interchangeable. For a product costing $60 and selling for $100, the $40 gross profit represents a 40% gross margin but a 66.67% markup. This distinction is documented in Shopify's 2026 markup-versus-margin guide.

Margin and markup formulas

Gross profit per unit = Net selling price − Direct cost per unit
Gross margin (%) = Gross profit / Net selling price × 100
Markup (%) = Gross profit / Direct cost × 100
Net selling price = List price × (1 − Discount / 100)
Price for target margin = Direct cost / (1 − Target margin / 100)
List price for target margin = Price for target margin / (1 − Discount / 100)
Price for target markup = Direct cost × (1 + Target markup / 100)
Margin from markup = Markup / (100 + Markup) × 100
Markup from margin = Margin / (100 − Margin) × 100

Direct cost in this tool includes product cost, per-unit shipping/packaging and fixed per-unit fees. The result is a gross contribution measure, not net profit after payroll, rent, advertising, interest or income tax. Percentage-based payment fees are not automatically modeled: convert them into a per-unit amount for a given price scenario.

Worked example: target 40% gross margin

A seller pays $60 for an item, $5 for shipping and $3 in direct fees. The total direct cost is $68. With a 40% target gross margin, the required discounted selling price is $68 ÷ 0.60 = $113.33. If the store offers a 10% discount, the list price must be approximately $125.93 to preserve that target. By comparison, applying a 40% markup to $68 produces a selling price of just $95.20 before any discount—a very different result.

How discounts affect gross margin

Discounts reduce revenue directly while unit cost often remains fixed. A 10% discount is not the same as a 10-percentage-point reduction in margin. International pricing and tax considerations

Use USD, EUR, GBP, INR, CAD, AUD, SGD or AED to label consistent monetary inputs. The calculator does not convert exchange rates or infer a jurisdiction's tax laws. Sales tax, GST or VAT may be handled differently by location, product category and customer. This model assumes the entered rate is added to the post-discount selling price, and the tax is collected on behalf of a government rather than treated as business revenue. Do not use tax-inclusive prices as pre-tax prices.

When is markup better than margin?

Markup is useful for quick cost-plus pricing; margin is useful for evaluating the share of sales revenue left after direct costs. For wholesale and retail pricing, calculate both and verify the target margin after shipping, packaging, marketplace fees and promotional discounts. High markup alone does not guarantee adequate net profitability.

Frequently asked questions

Is 40% markup the same as 40% margin?

No. A 40% markup means gross margin of approximately 28.57%. A 40% margin requires approximately 66.67% markup.

Can gross margin be negative?

Yes. When discounted selling price is below the unit's direct cost, gross profit and gross margin are negative. This calculator shows negative values and a loss warning.

Does the calculator include sales tax?

It adds a user-entered rate to the discounted price as a separate customer total. Tax is excluded from business gross revenue and margin. The user must determine the correct jurisdiction-specific rate.

Does it calculate net profit?

No. It calculates gross profit after the entered direct unit costs, before overhead and income taxes. Use a full profit-and-loss model for net profit.

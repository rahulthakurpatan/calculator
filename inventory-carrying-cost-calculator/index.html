What is inventory carrying cost?

Inventory carrying cost is the annual economic cost of keeping goods in stock rather than immediately selling or consuming them. It includes the cost of capital tied up in stock, variable warehouse expenses, insurance, inventory taxes where applicable, shrinkage and obsolescence. Fixed warehouse costs may also be allocated to stock for reporting, but they do not automatically fall when inventory declines.

How the calculator works

Variable annual cost = average inventory value × (capital + storage + insurance + risk) / 100 Total annual carrying cost = variable annual cost + fixed warehouse costs Effective carrying rate = annual cost / average inventory value × 100 Annual cost per average unit = annual carrying cost / average units New annual cost = inventory value × (1 − reduction%) × variable rate + fixed cost × (1 − avoidable%) Estimated savings = current annual cost − new annual cost

All percentages are annual percentages applied to the same cost-valued average inventory base. Fixed expenses are entered in currency per year and kept separate to avoid the common mistake of assuming every dollar of warehouse rent disappears when stock drops.

Worked example: $100,000 of inventory

Assume $100,000 average inventory, 2,000 units, 9% capital cost, 5% variable storage, 2% insurance and 4% obsolescence risk. The variable rate is 20% and variable carrying cost is $20,000. Add $6,000 of fixed warehouse expense for a total $26,000 per year, or 26% of average inventory value. Monthly cost is $2,166.67 and annual cost per unit is $13. If average inventory drops 20% but fixed warehouse cost stays unchanged, the new annual cost is $22,000 and the modeled annual saving is $4,000, not $5,200.

Why distinguish avoidable from fixed costs?

Some expenses, such as financing tied to stock or value-linked insurance, often vary with inventory. Others, such as a contracted warehouse lease, remain until capacity is actually released. This model lets you enter the portion of fixed expense you expect to avoid after the proposed stock reduction. Use zero unless there is a defensible operational change. Avoid double counting financing interest and the opportunity cost of the same capital.

Using holding cost in economic order quantity

The EOQ calculator needs annual holding cost per unit, usually called H. For a variable-cost-only EOQ comparison, use variable annual carrying rate times average cost per unit. The displayed all-in per-unit cost includes fixed overhead and may not be appropriate as the marginal H for EOQ unless the overhead truly changes with stock levels. Data quality and limitations

Use a monthly or weekly average inventory balance when seasonality makes opening and closing values misleading. For insurance, tax and obsolescence inputs, use actual company records where possible; tax treatment differs by jurisdiction. If a supplier offers volume discounts, cutting inventory may raise unit purchase prices. Stock reduction is not always feasible for perishable or highly volatile products. The model does not predict actual sales, inflation, or working-capital financing terms.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good inventory carrying cost rate?

There is no universally correct percentage. The rate depends on financing, warehousing, product perishability and the way fixed costs are allocated. Use your own annual costs rather than treating an industry average as a target.

Is inventory carrying cost the same as cost of goods sold?

No. COGS is the cost of goods sold during the period. Carrying cost is the cost of keeping inventory on hand before sale or use.

Does reducing inventory always save the full carrying rate?

No. Only costs that actually scale down with inventory should be treated as variable. Fixed warehouse costs require a real change in capacity or contracts to be avoided.

Does this calculate cash released?

The scenario changes average inventory value, but cash release depends on the timing of purchasing, payments, and sales. It is not a cash-flow forecast.

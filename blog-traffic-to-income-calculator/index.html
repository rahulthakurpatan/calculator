How to use the blog earning calculator

  1. Enter monthly pageviews from Google Analytics or another analytics tool. Add monthly visitors if you want an estimated visitor target.
  2. Enter your actual Page RPM from your ad network. If you are not monetised yet, use an assumption only to explore possible scenarios.
  3. Open the advanced section if you earn from affiliate links, sponsored posts or digital products.
  4. Add your monthly operating costs. The result separates gross revenue from estimated net profit.
  5. Set a monthly profit goal to estimate how many pageviews may be needed at the same Page RPM and income mix.

You can use the calculator with only pageviews and Page RPM. All other income sources and cost fields are optional. The calculator runs in your browser; it does not need your analytics login or advertising account connection.

How blog income is calculated

Display ad revenue using Page RPM

Estimated ad revenue = (Monthly pageviews ÷ 1,000) × Page RPM

Page RPM is a reporting metric used to compare estimated earnings against pageviews. Google AdSense defines Page RPM as estimated earnings divided by pageviews, multiplied by 1,000. Your own reporting value is more useful than a generic rate because actual results depend on your site and audience. Read Google's Page RPM explanation.

Affiliate income

Eligible pageviews × affiliate click rate × conversion rate × commission

For example, a pageview is counted as eligible only if it belongs to a page where you have an affiliate link. The calculator applies your click rate to those pageviews, then applies the conversion rate to estimated clicks. Use your affiliate dashboard data if available.

Sponsored posts and digital products

Sponsored posts × fee per post
Product sales × net income per sale

These income sources are entered directly because sponsorship rates and product margins vary widely. Enter the amount you expect to receive, not an unsupported industry average.

Net profit

Net profit = Total gross revenue − Monthly expenses

This is a simple operating estimate. It does not automatically calculate income tax, GST, refunds, payment processing fees or one-time expenses unless you include them in the relevant cost fields.

Pageviews, visitors and sessions are not the same

A visitor is a person or user recognised by your analytics system. A session is a visit during which that user interacts with your site. A pageview is recorded when a page is viewed. One visitor can create multiple sessions and multiple pageviews.

Display ad estimates in this tool use pageviews because Page RPM is calculated per 1,000 pageviews. Affiliate estimates use eligible pageviews and click behaviour. Do not multiply visitors by RPM unless you first convert visitors into pageviews using a realistic pages-per-visitor figure.

Why blog earnings vary so much

  • Audience location: Advertiser demand and visitor purchasing power differ across countries and markets.
  • Topic: Commercial intent, advertiser competition and the type of content can influence monetisation.
  • Ad setup: Ad formats, viewability, page experience and ad availability can affect results.
  • Seasonality: Advertiser budgets and demand can change during the year.
  • Affiliate fit: Relevant recommendations and a good match between readers and products matter more than traffic alone.
  • Costs: A site with higher revenue can still have lower profit if content, tools and marketing costs are high.

For these reasons, there is no reliable universal answer to how much a blog earns from 1,000 or 10,000 pageviews. Use your own historical data and treat projections as scenarios rather than promises.

How much traffic do you need to earn ₹10,000 a month?

The answer depends on the share of income coming from advertising, affiliate commissions, sponsorships and products, as well as your operating costs. If advertising is your only income source, the basic calculation is:

Required pageviews = (Monthly income goal ÷ Page RPM) × 1,000

When you include other income and expenses, the calculator first subtracts your non-ad income and adds your expenses to the target profit. It then estimates the ad revenue still needed and converts that amount into pageviews using your Page RPM. If the other income already covers the goal and costs, no additional ad traffic is required by this simplified model.

This calculation assumes that your Page RPM and affiliate click/conversion rates remain unchanged as traffic grows. In real life, audience mix, ad demand and conversion rates can change when a site scales.

The 12-month projection starts with your current monthly pageviews and compounds them using the monthly traffic growth rate you enter. Actual RPM, affiliate conversion, expenses and traffic growth can change from month to month, so review the assumptions regularly.

Use actual affiliate EPM when available

If your affiliate dashboard reports earnings per 1,000 eligible pageviews, choose the EPM option. Otherwise, use the click-and-sale funnel. Do not enter the same affiliate earnings in both methods. EPM is applied only to the percentage of pageviews you mark as eligible for affiliate links.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a blog earn per 1,000 pageviews?

There is no fixed amount. Earnings depend on your ad network, audience location, topic, ad setup and monetisation mix. Use your own Page RPM whenever possible instead of relying on generic earning claims.

How much traffic do I need to earn ₹10,000 per month from a blog?

It depends on your Page RPM, affiliate income, sponsorships, product sales and costs. Enter those details and set a ₹10,000 profit goal to estimate the required pageviews.

Is blog income based on visitors or pageviews?

Display ad Page RPM uses pageviews. Affiliate income depends on eligible pageviews, link clicks, conversions and commission. Visitors and pageviews should not be treated as interchangeable.

What is a good Page RPM?

There is no single good RPM for every site. Compare your RPM over similar periods and traffic mixes, and use your own data to plan.

Can a new blog earn without AdSense?

Some blogs earn through affiliate programmes, sponsorships, services, memberships or digital products. These options depend on audience trust, relevant content, programme approval and actual conversions.

Does US traffic always earn more than Indian traffic?

Not always. Audience location can influence advertiser demand, but topic, visitor intent, ad setup and seasonality also matter. Compare your own analytics rather than assuming a fixed country multiplier.

Does this calculator show guaranteed earnings?

No. It estimates possible revenue from the values you enter. Actual earnings can be different due to ad delivery, conversions, refunds, attribution, invalid traffic adjustments and platform rules.

What is the difference between revenue and profit?

Revenue is income before expenses. Profit is what remains after subtracting your operating costs. This tool shows both so you can avoid treating gross earnings as take-home income.

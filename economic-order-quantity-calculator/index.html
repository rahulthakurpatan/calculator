What is economic order quantity (EOQ)?

EOQ is the order size that minimizes annual fixed ordering costs plus average cycle-stock holding costs for an item with steady demand. Ordering too frequently raises processing and fixed freight costs; ordering too much raises warehouse, insurance, spoilage and financing costs. The theoretical optimum balances those costs. Our practical recommendation also checks supplier pack sizes and minimum order quantities rather than rounding blindly.

Theoretical EOQ (Q*) = √(2 × D × S / H) D = annual demand in units; S = fixed cost per order H = annual holding cost per unit Annual ordering cost = D / Q × S Annual cycle-stock holding cost = Q / 2 × H Annual relevant cost = ordering cost + cycle-stock holding cost Orders per year = D / Q Operating days between orders = operating days × Q / D Reorder point = D / operating days × lead time + safety stock

Worked example: 10,000 units per year

Suppose annual demand is 10,000 units, fixed ordering cost is $50 and annual holding cost is $2 per unit. The theoretical EOQ is √(2 × 10,000 × 50 / 2) = 707.11 units. At the theoretical optimum, annual ordering cost and holding cost are both about $707.11, totaling $1,414.21. If your supplier ships cartons of 24 and requires at least 24 units, compare 696 and 720 units rather than arbitrarily rounding the EOQ. With a current order size of 1,000, annual relevant cost is $1,500, so moving near the EOQ may reduce modeled annual cost by roughly $86. The exact feasible recommendation depends on pack and minimum inputs.

How supplier pack sizes change the answer

The exact EOQ can be fractional, but real orders often must be whole cartons or pallets. The calculator tests the feasible multiples immediately around the theoretical optimum, plus the minimum permitted quantity, and selects the one with the lowest annual ordering-plus-holding cost. It assumes all order quantities must be multiples of the entered pack size. Quantity discounts, truckload thresholds, storage capacity and expiry limits are not included; those may change the best real-world decision.

When should you reorder?

EOQ answers how much to order, not when. Reorder point uses average daily demand times lead time plus separately estimated safety stock. For 10,000 annual units, 365 operating days, seven operating days of lead time and 100 units of safety stock, the simple reorder point is approximately 292 units (rounded up). Actual lead-time variation and uncertain demand need a statistically justified safety-stock policy.

Cost savings are estimates

Reported savings compare your current entered order quantity with the feasible recommended quantity, using only fixed order processing and average cycle-stock holding costs. Purchase cost is omitted because it is constant across order sizes in this model. Savings do not include implementation, discounts, changing freight tariffs, stockouts or changes in sales. Safety stock is not included in the cycle-stock cost comparison because it is assumed unchanged across scenarios.

Common questions

What is the difference between EOQ and reorder point?

EOQ determines an economical replenishment quantity; reorder point determines the inventory threshold at which to place a new order. Use both together.

Can I enter monthly demand?

Convert monthly demand to annual demand first. If you typically sell 800 units per month, enter about 9,600 annual units, provided that demand is reasonably stable.

Does a larger EOQ always save money?

No. Larger orders reduce order frequency but increase average cycle stock. The combined cost falls only up to the optimum and rises beyond it.

What if holding cost or ordering cost is zero?

The standard EOQ formula assumes both are positive. This calculator rejects zero values rather than presenting a misleading finite optimum.
